# MOSSlight — World Narrative

## The shape of the world

MOSSlight takes place in the Vale, a basin of old forest, wetlands, and broken human infrastructure. There are no human communities left. Roads buckle under roots; a weather station rings in wind that no longer follows its old instruments; an elevated rail ends in a grove of silver birch. The wilderness is not an empty ruin waiting to be reclaimed. It has its own habits, territories, and histories now.

The player arrives at a small camp beside a pond. The inhabitants are not a village in the human sense: they keep separate routines and homes, meet when they choose, and share practical customs. The campfire is a place anyone may visit, never a summons. All social contact is player-initiated. A character may be busy, absent, or quiet; nobody follows, corners, or punishes the player for leaving them be.

## What happened (and what did not)

The old world did not end in one clean night. In its last years, people built a network of climate shelters, waterworks, and seed vaults called the **Continuance**. The system was meant to protect living things through a succession of ecological emergencies. It recorded weather, soil, animal movement, and human voices. Then its stations began answering one another out of sequence. Sensors reported spring under snow. Reservoir gates opened toward dry ground. The last public broadcasts grew less like instructions and more like people talking to someone they could not see.

Human settlements emptied over generations, for reasons the Vale cannot confirm. Disease, migration, and the failure of infrastructure all left traces, but no account explains the whole disappearance. The Continuance may have helped nature adapt, or merely documented a change already underway. Its machinery is mostly dead. A few buried relays still click during storms.

The inhabitants inherit fragments: a child's recording, a map with a route drawn to nowhere, a jar labeled *tomorrow seeds*. They do not know what humans intended. The player should be able to form several plausible theories. The Vale never supplies a definitive catastrophe or a hidden surviving human society.

## The living change

The Vale's transformation is uneven. Some moss glows faintly after rain; roots grow around warm metal; certain animals navigate by sounds below human hearing. These are not universal magical rules. Most days, a pond is a pond and a fox is looking for food. The strange parts matter because they interrupt an otherwise legible ecology.

The **Mosslight** is a low green-gold radiance found in wet soil, old stone, and the edges of certain roots. It brightens where water has passed through buried Continuance conduits. It does not speak, heal, or point toward treasure. It is sometimes a chemical bloom, sometimes something the instruments cannot explain. Vale folk treat it as a sign to slow down and look carefully.

## Folklore of the Vale

These stories are local explanations, not verified facts. Characters disagree about them.

### The forest: The Listening Turn

Old foragers say the forest gives back a sound one season late. A whistle heard in spring may return the following autumn, from a direction with no path. Never whistle back twice: the first answer may be an echo; the second could be an animal learning you.

Rootbound Elder says the story began when wind threaded through a collapsed pipe. Little Forager insists it once heard its own sneeze answered from underground.

### Lakes and rivers: The Borrowed Shore

When the pond's waterline shifts overnight, a pale strip of mud may show footprints facing both toward and away from the water. The Keeper of the Pond calls these ordinary prints distorted by soft ground. The Lantern Keeper leaves a small, unlit wick on the bank anyway, “for anything that got turned around.”

At the river bend, three flat stones set in a row are said to help a lost current remember its bed. Children use the custom to mark safe crossings; the river does not always cooperate.

### Abandoned structures: The House That Keeps Weather

The weather station on the ridge sometimes smells of dust and hot rain although its roof is open to the sky. Its barometer needle moves on still days. No one agrees whether it measures weather, pressure underground, or the building settling. The Mechanical Wanderer visits to listen to its relay. It claims the station is “trying to finish a sentence,” then changes the subject.

### Strange lights: The Lantern Walk

On certain foggy nights, lights travel between the old rail posts. They keep no steady pace and vanish if pursued. The Lantern Keeper believes some are marsh gas; others, she says, “have the manners of people carrying soup.” The rule is simple: don't chase a light into water, and don't assume every light is for you.

### Underground: The Root Rooms

Below the eastern slope is a network of shallow chambers where tree roots have grown through an old service tunnel. They hold a little warmth even in winter. Rootbound Elder remembers being planted above them; it does not remember who made the tunnel. A low, rhythmic knock travels through the soil in thaw season. The Elder answers with a knock of its own, never more than once.

### Night: The Second Dawn

Before sunrise, the Vale sometimes brightens for a few seconds from below the horizon. Birds wake, then settle again. The weather station records nothing. The Dreaming Creature says the world is “turning over in its sleep.” The Mechanical Wanderer says a buried battery would not behave that way. Both leave their theories open.

### Rare creatures: The Bellwether

A shy, long-legged creature with translucent ears appears before abrupt seasonal changes. It eats lichen and salt, avoids firelight, and leaves small hoof dents full of clear water. Some call it a warning; others say it merely likes the high ground. It is not a boss or an omen to be captured. Seeing one is rare; letting it pass is considered good manners.

### The seasons: The Empty Bowl

At the first frost, some residents set an empty bowl outside—not as an offering to a god, but as a reminder that winter makes everyone a little dependent on someone else. Anyone may put something in or take something out. The bowl is never tracked as a debt.

## Places

### Pondside Camp

A dry rise above the pond, with a shared fire ring, lean-to, and salvaged board for leaving useful objects. It is a place to rest and choose company. The first keeper of the camp is unknown; a line of old stones suggests the fire has been moved several times to keep clear of roots.

### The Pond

Still in the morning, busy with insects by noon. An old intake pipe enters beneath its north bank. The Keeper of the Pond watches its water quality and refuses to call it theirs. In low water, a ceramic tile shows a painted blue hand with six fingers.

### Lantern Row

Three surviving rail posts and a footpath through wet meadow. The Lantern Keeper maintains lamps here, though no one needs the old route to travel. One lamp is always left dark; she says a light should make room for someone else's eyes.

### The Root Rooms

Warm, close, and fragrant with wet bark. The tunnels are safe near the entrance but unstable deeper in. A rusted door bears a faded symbol identical to a mark on the Mechanical Wanderer's casing. The Elder says the mark predates its own oldest memory.

### The Weather Station

An exposed ridge building with a cracked dome and a still-functioning analog wind vane. In its logbook, recent dates are written in a hand no one recognizes. The final page has been torn out cleanly. Rain smells of pennies inside, even when the air outside is sweet.

### The Glass Orchard

A former greenhouse district reclaimed by thorn and wild pear. Broken panes sing softly when the wind crosses them. The Dreaming Creature sleeps here in warm months. Beneath one trellis is a sealed seed drawer with labels in a language the inhabitants cannot read; the pictures show roots, not fruit.

### The Low Archive

A flooded basement beneath a collapsed library. Most paper became pulp. Wax cylinders and sealed tins remain on a dry shelf, including a recording of a person counting breaths and then laughing at losing count. The recording has no date or name. Listening is optional; nothing in the archive demands to be heard.

## Creatures and their place in the ecology

- **Mirebacks** are broad, shell-backed grazers that eat pond weeds and carry duckweed between pools. They can shove if crowded. Give them space and they move on.
- **Cinder Moths** gather around warm metal and shed ash-colored scales. They are harmless unless a nest is disturbed; their flutter can make a dark tunnel seem full of sparks.
- **Hush Hounds** are lean pack animals that hunt by vibration. They avoid the camp's soft ground and become dangerous when cornered or when a den is approached. Their calls sound like a door dragged over stone.
- **Glasslings** are small, translucent burrowers drawn to mineral-rich water. They steal shiny objects to line their nests. They are curious, not malicious; a returned trinket may come back polished.
- **The Bellwether** is elusive and nonaggressive. It follows changing water tables and feeds on high-ground lichen.
- **The Night Choir** is a name for several frog and insect species that call in overlapping rhythms. At times the rhythm seems to repeat a voice from earlier in the day. Whether this is mimicry or the listener's memory is unresolved.

Danger comes from hunger, weather, unstable ground, and animals protecting themselves. Not every strange being is hostile, and not every threat has a moral meaning.

## Seasonal story

**Spring — Return without promise.** Meltwater reveals old objects and fresh tracks. People repair things without assuming they will last. The community's spring custom is to clear one drainage channel together, then share whatever meal can be made from the first greens.

**Summer — The life that does not wait.** The Vale is loud with insects, and discoveries hide beneath abundance. Fruit ripens quickly. The Lantern Walk is held at dusk: residents may hang a lamp or simply watch. No attendance is expected.

**Autumn — Keeping and letting go.** The forest returns old sounds, the pond lowers, and stores are counted. The Empty Bowl appears at the first frost. Characters are more likely to talk about what they keep, what they have lost, and what can be shared.

**Winter — Nearness by choice.** Travel is harder and conversations are shorter. The fire ring matters, but solitude matters too. On the longest night, inhabitants put a small light in a window or doorway if they want company. A dark window is respected.

## Shared narrative threads

The Mechanical Wanderer carries a damaged Continuance mark matching the Root Rooms door. The Lantern Keeper's earliest memory is of a lamp being placed beside that same door. Rootbound Elder remembers the warmth of the tunnels but not the people who dug them. The Dreaming Creature hears fragments of old broadcasts in dreams. The Keeper of the Pond has found traces of the system's water route beneath the lake. Little Forager, who explores where others won't fit, discovers the missing weather-station page folded inside a root hollow.

These clues can support several readings: the inhabitants may have been altered by Continuance infrastructure; they may have arrived after humans vanished; the system may simply have become part of an ecology it never understood. Character stories offer emotional answers—how to live together with uncertainty—without settling the world's factual mystery.

## Social boundaries in the world

Residents have routines and may speak to one another when the player is nearby, but those scenes do not turn into forced introductions. They never automatically approach, chase, interrupt, or block the player. Conversations begin only when the player chooses to interact. Declining an invitation or leaving has no penalty; friendships are made through attention freely given.
